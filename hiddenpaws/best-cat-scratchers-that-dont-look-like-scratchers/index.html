The reason most attractive scratchers fail is height, not taste. Cats scratch to stretch the whole body, so a 17-inch sculpture in oak veneer is an object your cat walks past on the way to the sofa arm. The 2016 owner survey in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery found cats scratched their preferred substrate more often when the post was a simple upright or a tree at least three feet high.

So this guide sorts pretty scratchers by whether they clear that bar, and says plainly which ones do not. Two of the five are under three feet and are here anyway, because a board the cat uses beside the sofa beats a tall post in the spare room — and International Cat Care's advice for small homes is to fix a panel to the wall, which is exactly what one of these does.

How to shop for a scratcher you can leave in the living room

Height for a full stretch
The ASPCA's figure is at least three feet. Here the 36-inch Tuft + Paw Zip and the 32-inch SmartCat post clear it, the sculptural pieces do not, and the difference shows up in which one the cat uses.
It cannot move when scratched
International Cat Care notes that products too light to support scratching, or that cannot be fixed to something rigid, move during use and get rejected. A 16.9 lb post on a 16-inch base does not move.
The right surface
Ohio State's guidance is that most cats prefer a rough material they can shred, and names sisal as ideal. The 2016 owner survey found rope was the most-used substrate when offered, while carpet was the most commonly offered. Cardboard suits kittens and older cats.
Replaceable parts
The scratching surface dies first. Hepper and Tuft + Paw sell replacement cardboard or parts; Pioneer Pet sells base and top pieces but not sisal, because the sisal is built into the post during manufacture.
No exposed staples
Two brands here call it out — the fabric ends are tucked inside the post — and it separates a ten-year post from one that snags a claw in year two.
Whether it earns its floor space
In a small apartment a scratcher competes with furniture. The ones that win hang on a wall, double as a bed, or look enough like an object that you stop noticing it is for the cat.

Questions people ask

How tall should a cat scratching post be?

The ASPCA's figure is at least three feet, sturdy enough not to wobble, in a rough material such as sisal or burlap. International Cat Care puts it functionally: as tall as your cat standing on its hind legs. Only two picks here clear that bar — a 36-inch post and a 32-inch one.

Sisal, carpet or cardboard — which do cats prefer?

The 2016 owner survey found rope was the substrate most often used when offered, even though carpet was the material most commonly offered. Ohio State names sisal as ideal because it is satisfying to scratch and survives repeated use. Cardboard has a place too: International Cat Care notes kittens and senior cats often prefer it, because it offers less resistance.

How many scratchers does one cat need?

International Cat Care recommends one per cat plus one extra, in different locations. Ohio State adds that you want both vertical and horizontal options, because a cat that shreds the rug and one that shreds the chair leg are telling you two different things.

Can a scratcher go on the wall?

Yes, and International Cat Care specifically recommends it when space is tight — scratching panels fixed to walls, in sisal twine, bark or corrugated cardboard. The Tab in this guide is designed for exactly that, with its own aluminum mount, and it frees the floor entirely.

My cat ignores the new scratcher. What now?

Move it to where the cat is already scratching, not where you want it. The ASPCA's advice is to place posts next to the objects being damaged, offer different surfaces and orientations until you find the preference, then add more of that kind. And do not throw out a shabby post: cats prefer shredded objects because they can get their claws in.

Does a cat tree count as a scratching post?

It can. The 2016 survey found cats scratched their preferred substrate more often when the post was a simple upright type or a cat tree with two or more levels and at least three feet high — so a tall tree with real sisal posts does the job, and a short tree wrapped in carpet mostly does not.
