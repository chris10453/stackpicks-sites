A covered bed is not a luxury item, it is the thing the veterinary guidance keeps asking for. The 2013 AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines open with a safe place, described as a private and secure area, often in a raised location, that gives the cat a sense of enclosure. A cardboard box does it. So does a cube you can leave in a living room.

The choice below is really between three shapes: a hard-sided cube with a lookout on top, a soft cave you can carry to the sun, and a low open cave for a cat that no longer jumps. Every dimension here comes from the manufacturer's own page, because “fits most cats” is not a size.

How to shop for a covered cat bed

Published external footprint
This object lives in the room permanently. Nineteen and a half inches square is a large side table; fourteen inches is a cushion. The gap between the two is most of a small flat's spare corner.
Whether the cat can also get on top
A covered bed that is only covered gives a cat one option. A cube with a hammock or a platform on the roof gives it two — hide when the vacuum comes out, watch the room when it does not.
Whether the cushion comes out and washes
The cushion is the part that absorbs everything. Catit publishes that the Vesper cushions are removable; TRIXIE publishes a removable insert; Armarkat says the whole bed is machine washable. A sewn-in pad with no washing instruction makes the bed a consumable.
Material, honestly stated
MDF with laminate, velour polyester, felted plush — each wears and smells differently. The guides here quote the maker's own material line rather than describing a photograph.
How tall the doorway sits
A cut-out door eight inches off the floor is nothing to a young cat and a decision to an old one. The picks here run from a lipless open mouth to a cube a cat steps up into, and that is the difference between a bed used daily and a bed used once.
What it looks like switched off
Cat furniture is judged from the sofa. A bleached-oak cube reads as storage; a fur dome reads as a pet bed. Both are fine — just decide which one you are buying before it arrives.

Questions people ask

Do cats actually prefer covered beds?

Many do, and the veterinary guidance explains why. The AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines describe a safe place as private and secure, often raised, giving a sense of enclosure — and note that a cat feels safer if it cannot see a potential threat, even when its whole body is not concealed. That is a description of a covered bed with a doorway.

How big should a covered cat bed be?

Big enough that the cat can turn around and lie fully stretched inside. The sleeping areas here run from a 14-inch square tent up to a 27.6-inch cave, and a cat that has to fold to fit will use it for a nap and not for a night.

Should a covered bed be up high or on the floor?

Both, if you can manage it. The guidelines ask for a minimum of two resting areas in separate locations, and Ohio State's Indoor Pet Initiative notes that cats may feel safer sleeping somewhere high. A covered bed on the floor plus a raised perch elsewhere covers both moods.

How do you get a cat to use a new bed?

Put it where the cat already sleeps rather than where you want it to sleep. Ohio State's advice on resting areas is to watch the cat, because it will show you where it feels comfortable, and to move it to a new spot only with treats and patience. A perfectly good bed in the wrong corner is the most common reason one goes unused.
