Beige carpet towers exist because carpet is cheap to staple, and they are why cat furniture gets exiled to a spare room — which defeats the point, because a cat wants height where the household is. Every tower below is compared on the three numbers that decide whether it can stand in a living room: footprint, height and published capacity.

Capacity is the spec this category hides hardest: of the six picks here, only two publish a limit at all. Where a brand is silent the table shows a dash. If you have a 16 lb cat, or two cats who chase each other up the thing, that silence is a reason to shortlist the two towers that print a number.

How to shop for a cat tree that looks like furniture

Footprint before height
Height is free in an apartment; floor is not. Footprints here run from 15.75 x 19.69 inches to 35 x 25.2, the difference between fitting beside a sofa and replacing an armchair.
A published weight limit
Two brands here publish one — 16 lb per cat on the Mau Rizzo, at least 30 lb per platform on the Lotus. Everyone else leaves you guessing, which matters most for large breeds.
Materials that survive claws and look like furniture
Wood veneer over plywood, laminated MDF, powder-coated steel and bamboo all read as furniture at three paces. Sisal and seagrass are what the cat wants; carpet is what the factory wants.
Replaceable parts
The scratching post dies first. Catit, The Refined Feline, Omlet and Mau all publish replacement parts, which turns a five-year purchase into a ten-year one.
Stability, and whether it can be anchored
A tall tower on a small base moves when a cat lands on top. The Metropolitan is the only pick here with a published wall anchor; the Lotus uses floor levellers and a reinforced support bar instead.
Somewhere to hide, not just somewhere to sit
Ohio State describes a cat's preferred resting places as quiet spots away from the rest of the family, and a den does that in a way an open platform never will.

Questions people ask

How tall does a cat tree actually need to be?

Tall enough to beat the furniture it competes with. A 30-inch tower is a perch beside a sofa; a 62 to 69-inch tower puts a cat above human head height. Ohio State describes a perch as anything that lets a cat lie, sit, sleep or look outside from above, and recommends more than one.

Do cat trees need to be anchored to the wall?

Only one product here publishes an anchor: the Metropolitan ships an optional wall anchor for active multi-cat homes. Tall, narrow, light towers are the ones that tip, and a 62-inch frame 15 inches deep is that shape. Heavier towers — the 72 lb Lotus — use mass and floor levellers instead.

Sisal, seagrass or carpet?

Ohio State's guidance is that most cats prefer a rough material they can shred, and names sisal as ideal. Five of the six towers here use sisal or seagrass; carpet snags claws and is used because it is cheap to staple.

How much floor space does a cat tree really take?

Between 15.75 x 19.69 inches and 35 x 25.2 inches here — a factor of nearly three. Add six inches of clearance on any side with an overhanging platform, because a cat landing on a cantilevered perch needs room to miss.

Will two cats share one tree?

More readily if it has more than one den entrance and platforms at different heights. International Cat Care notes that vertical space lets cats in multi-cat homes stay off the ground and away from each other — practically, two levels far enough apart that neither cat is in the other's face.
