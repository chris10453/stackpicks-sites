Beige carpet towers exist because carpet is cheap to staple, and they are why cat furniture gets exiled to a spare room — which defeats the point, because a cat wants height where the household is. Every tower below is compared on the three numbers that decide whether it can stand in a living room: footprint, height and published capacity.

Capacity is the spec this category hides hardest, and the four towers that print one do not print the same figure. Two rate the structure (30 lb per platform on the Lotus, 30 lb of holding weight on the Armarkat) and two rate the animal (23 lb per cat on the Cento, 16 lb on the Rizzo). The Metropolitan and the Omlet publish nothing at all, and the table says so rather than showing a dash.

How to shop for a cat tree that looks like furniture

Footprint before height
Height is free in an apartment; floor is not. Footprints here run from 16 inches square to 31 by 15 inches — 256 square inches against 465.
A published weight limit
Four of these six towers publish one, in two different units, and the unit matters more than the number. A per-platform load tells you what a landing cat will not break. A per-cat rating tells you whether your cat is the wrong customer.
Materials that survive claws and look like furniture
Wood veneer over plywood, solid Scots pine, powder-coated steel and bamboo all read as furniture at three paces. Sisal and jute are what the cat wants; carpet is what the factory wants.
Replaceable parts
The scratching post dies first. The Refined Feline, Armarkat, Omlet and Mau all publish replacement parts, and Armarkat and Mau sell them by model number, which turns a five-year purchase into a ten-year one.
Stability, and whether it can be anchored
A tall tower on a small base moves when a cat lands on top. The Metropolitan is the only pick here with a published wall anchor; the Lotus uses floor levellers instead.
Somewhere to hide, not just somewhere to sit
Ohio State describes a cat's preferred resting places as quiet spots away from the rest of the family, and a den does that in a way an open platform never will. Three towers here have one.

Questions people ask

How tall does a cat tree actually need to be?

Tall enough to beat the furniture it competes with. A 29-inch tower is a perch beside a sofa; a 62 to 69-inch tower puts a cat above human head height. Ohio State describes a perch as anything that lets a cat lie, sit, sleep or look outside from above, and recommends more than one.

Do cat trees need to be anchored to the wall?

Only one product here publishes an anchor: the Metropolitan ships an optional wall anchor for active multi-cat homes. Tall, narrow, light towers are the ones that tip, and a 62-inch frame 15 inches deep is that shape. Heavier towers — the 72 lb Lotus — use mass and floor levellers instead.

Sisal, jute or carpet?

Ohio State's guidance is that most cats prefer a rough material they can shred, and names sisal as ideal. Every tower on this page ships a rope, sisal or cardboard scratching surface of some kind; carpet snags claws and is used because it is cheap to staple.

How much floor space does a cat tree really take?

Between 16 inches square and 31 by 15 inches here — 256 square inches against 465, not quite a factor of two. Add six inches of clearance on any side with an overhanging platform, because a cat landing on a cantilevered perch needs room to miss.

Will two cats share one tree?

More readily if it offers resting places at different heights, far enough apart that neither cat is in the other's face. International Cat Care notes that vertical space lets cats in multi-cat homes stay off the ground and away from each other. The Cento's floor-level cave and raised basket are that arrangement inside one footprint.
