How to introduce a second cat in a small apartment — a step-by-step guide

By Chris Y. · Two cats, one-bedroom apartment; measures the inside of everything Updated September 12, 2026

The introduction protocol every shelter and veterinary source describes needs one thing a small flat has least of — a room you can close. Everything else in it is free, and the part people get wrong is not the technique, it is the speed.

The ASPCA's guidance is to separate the cats in different rooms for several days or weeks with separate beds, bowls and litter boxes, to feed them on opposite sides of a closed door, to swap the rooms each day so both get access to the other's scent, and only then to crack that door open one inch.

This page takes those steps and works out what they mean in 700 square feet: which room to use, how to run a scent swap when the flat has one bathroom, how many litter boxes a two-cat flat actually needs, and what to do when the only closable door is the one you sleep behind.

Questions people ask

How long does it take to introduce two cats?

Plan for two to four weeks and be ready for longer. The ASPCA describes separating cats for several days or weeks before the first door opening, and says that where there has been serious aggression the reintroduction should go at a much slower pace — several days to a few weeks — with daily sessions that gradually move the cats closer.

How many litter boxes do two cats need in a flat?

Three is the usual veterinary rule — one per cat plus one — and the AAFP and ISFM environmental guidelines ask for a minimum of two toileting areas in separate locations for a multi-cat home. In a one-bedroom flat that usually means one in the bathroom, one in a hallway cupboard or an enclosure, and a third somewhere the cats do not have to pass each other to reach.

What if I only have one room with a door?

Use the bathroom for the first days if it is the only option, then swap the cats between the bathroom and the rest of the flat each day so both animals get the other's scent through the whole space. The daily room swap is part of the ASPCA protocol, not an improvisation — it is how each cat learns the other's smell without a face-to-face meeting.

When can I leave them alone together?

Only when they can peacefully eat and play within a couple of feet of each other, which is the ASPCA's explicit threshold. Start with short unsupervised periods and lengthen them gradually rather than leaving for a full working day the first time it goes well.

What does it mean if they hiss at the door?

It means the door opened too early. Growling, spitting, hissing and swatting are the signs the guidance names, and the instruction is to go back a step and restart at a slower pace. Hissing in the first week is information, not failure.

Should I get a second cat at all in a small flat?

Often yes, if the resources are separated. The environmental guidelines are more concerned with whether each cat can eat, drink, toilet and rest without having to look at the other than with square footage. Two cats in a well-arranged one-bedroom flat do better than two cats in a large house with one feeding station.

What the guidance actually says

Two documents carry most of the weight here. The ASPCA's guidance on aggression between household cats gives the mechanics of an introduction, and the AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines give the arrangement the flat has to end up in.

The ASPCA's sequence is specific. Separate the cats in different rooms for several days or weeks, with separate beds, bowls and litter boxes. Put the food bowls on opposite sides of the closed door so each cat associates the other's smell with dinner. Each day, swap which cat is in which room, so both experience the other's scent through the whole space. Rub each cat with a towel and bring the towel to the other cat. When both cats are relaxed, crack the door open one inch; if they stay calm, open it a little more, and a little more again. Leave them alone together only once they can peacefully eat and play within a couple of feet of each other, and build up the unsupervised time gradually.

If growling, spitting, hissing or swatting appears, the instruction is to go back and slow down. Where the aggression has been severe, separate them for longer and reintroduce at a much slower pace — several days to a few weeks of daily sessions that move the cats closer by degrees.

The environmental guidelines then describe what the flat should look like afterwards. Key resources — feeding, drinking, toileting, scratching, play and resting places — should be multiple and separated, with a minimum of two resting areas, two feeding areas and two toileting areas, each in its own location. The stated reason is that separated resources let individual cats avoid seeing each other, which reduces competition and stress. Pillar one asks for a private, secure, often raised place each cat can retreat to.

The flat, in order

Before the new cat arrives, work through the space rather than the schedule.

  1. Pick the closable room. A bedroom is better than a bathroom because it has soft surfaces that hold scent, but the bathroom works if it is what you have. The new cat lives there with its own litter box, food, water, bed and a hiding place for the first days.
  2. Count the litter boxes. Two cats want three boxes in at least two locations. In a one-bedroom flat, a litter box enclosure makes a second location possible in a living room without the room becoming about the litter box.
  3. Split the feeding stations. Two feeding areas in different places, not two bowls side by side. Our bowls guide covers the shallow dishes that stop a nervous cat guarding a station.
  4. Add a second high place. Vertical space is how two cats share a small floor. A shelf, a cleared bookcase top or a window perch gives the less confident cat somewhere to be above the other one — see how much vertical space a cat actually needs.

All four of these should be in place before the second cat comes through the door, because the first week is not the week to be assembling furniture.

Week by week

Treat this as the shape of the thing rather than a fixed calendar. Cats move at their own pace and the protocol's own instruction is to go back a step whenever the signs appear.

Days 1 to 3. The new cat stays in its room with the door shut. Nobody meets anybody. Feed both cats near the door, far enough away that neither stops eating, and move the bowls a little closer each meal.

Days 3 to 7. Start the daily room swap — the new cat explores the flat while the resident cat spends an hour or two in the closed room. This is the single most useful step in a small flat, because it does the work of scent exchange that a big house does by having more rooms. Add the towel swap: rub one cat, leave the towel with the other, and pair it with food.

Week 2. If both cats are eating calmly either side of the door and neither is guarding it, crack the door an inch. Two minutes is enough the first time. Repeat, widening only when both stay relaxed. A door wedge or a baby barrier lets you hold the gap at an inch without holding the door.

Week 3 and after. Short supervised sessions in the same room, with food and a wand toy so that being together predicts something good. Unsupervised time starts only when they eat and play within a couple of feet of each other, and starts short.

The small-flat problems nobody writes about

One bathroom. If the only closable door is the bathroom, use it for the first three days and then move the new cat to the bedroom at night and the bathroom by day, or reverse the arrangement so the resident cat does the swap. What matters is that both cats spend time in the space the other has scented.

Open-plan rooms. A studio has no second location in the usual sense, so make locations vertically instead. A perch, a shelf and a covered bed at three different heights are three places, even in one room.

A resident cat that guards the food. Move the bowls apart first, then feed at the same time in sight of each other at a distance where both keep eating. Competition at a single station is the most common cause of a two-cat problem in a small flat, and it is the easiest thing to fix.

Night-time. Keep them separated at night until they are relaxed together in the day. The middle of the night is when a defended doorway turns into a fight nobody supervises.

When to stop and ask a vet

An introduction that is going badly looks like a cat that has stopped eating, one that has stopped using the litter box, or fights that draw blood rather than noise. Any of those is a veterinary conversation rather than a furniture problem — medical causes are common and behavior advice built on a pain problem does not work. The same is true of a resident cat that starts spraying: it is a stress signal with a medical differential, and it needs the vet before it needs another litter box.

If the introduction simply stalls — two cats living parallel lives, no fighting but no contact either — that is a normal outcome and not a failure. Plenty of two-cat households run permanently on separated resources and mutual tolerance, and the environmental guidelines are written around exactly that.
