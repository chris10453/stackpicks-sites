The problem with buying for someone who already makes good coffee is that they own the obvious things. So this list avoids grinders, kettles and brewers and picks the small objects that sit next to them: a valve that turns an AeroPress into something else, a canister that removes air rather than trapping it, a hand grinder for the suitcase, a moka pot that publishes its own yield.

Everything here is chosen on a published figure rather than a description. If the person you are buying for has not got a grinder yet, buy that instead — our grinder guide is the better page.

How to shop for a coffee gift

They probably do not own it
Anybody who makes coffee at home owns a brewer and a mug. Nothing here is a brewer, a kettle or an electric grinder — the three things most likely to arrive as a duplicate.
The maker publishes a number
Every pick here comes with a figure from its own manufacturer — a diameter, a capacity, a filter size, a bean weight. A gift chosen on a published specification is one you can defend; a gift chosen on a product description is a gamble with someone else's counter space.
It gets used more than once
The failure mode of coffee gifts is the novelty — the kit used twice, the syrup set that goes sticky in a cupboard. Everything below has a daily or weekly job.
It does not need the rest of a system
One pick attaches to an AeroPress and one is a hand grinder, stated plainly. The other five work on their own.

Questions people ask

What if they already own a grinder and a brewer?

That is who this page is for. Nothing on it is a grinder, a kettle or a drip machine. The Prismo and the Mighty Small carafe in particular are things people who make good coffee know about and have not bought yet.

Which of these works without knowing what they already own?

The Airscape canister and the Carter Move mug. Everybody who buys whole beans needs somewhere to keep them, and almost everybody carries coffee out of the house. The Prismo is the opposite — it fits only the AeroPress Original, not the Go or the XL.

Is a hand grinder a good gift for someone with an electric one?

Only if they travel. A Skerton Pro is a second grinder for a suitcase or a desk, not an upgrade on a countertop machine. Hario publishes a 100 g capacity and a 167 by 95 by 195 mm body, which is a bag-sized object, and grinding 20 g by hand takes a minute or two of real work.

What size moka pot should I buy as a gift?

Six cups, unless you know they drink espresso-sized coffees. Bialetti's own table reads 3 cup = 4.4 oz and 6 cup = 9.1 oz, and 4.4 oz is a single small cup. Check the hob too — aluminum moka pots do nothing on induction without an adapter plate.

Is coffee itself a good gift?

Yes, with a roast date on the bag and bought within a week of giving it. Beans are at their best two to four weeks from roasting, so a bag bought in November for Christmas is a worse gift than any object on this page. Pair a fresh bag with the canister and it fixes itself.

Also considered

  • Pour-over starter kits — A dripper, a server, a scoop and a hundred filters in one box, usually at a discount on the parts. They are genuinely good value and they are the single most-duplicated coffee gift there is, because everyone who owns a dripper already owns the rest of it.
  • Electric milk frothers — Cheap, popular, and used about four times. A frother makes warm foamy milk, not textured milk, and the people who care about the difference already have a steam wand. The ones who do not care will stop using it by February.
  • Flavored syrup sets — The archetypal coffee gift that ends up sticky at the back of a cupboard. Nothing wrong with syrup; it is just not a gift for somebody whose hobby is tasting what the beans do.
  • A second AeroPress — Tempting, because it is the most-loved object in home coffee. It is also the one thing an enthusiast already owns, which is why the Prismo and the Go are on this page instead — one upgrades the Original they have, and the other is a different object rather than a duplicate.
  • Subscription boxes — A good idea executed badly more often than not — roast dates rarely published, bags arriving on a schedule rather than when the last one runs out. A single fresh bag from a roaster they like is better and cheaper.
