A pour-over setup is the cheapest real upgrade in home coffee and the smallest: a dripper, a filter and a kettle you can aim need about seven inches of counter and no clearance above at all. That makes this the guide to read if the drip machines all failed your cabinet measurement.

How to shop for a pour-over setup

Can you set a temperature, or only pick one?
SCA Standard 310-2021 specifies 90 to 96 °C — roughly 194 to 205 °F. A to-the-degree dial lets you move inside that band when a coffee tastes sour; presets get you into it and stop. Both beat a kettle that only boils, which lands above the standard.
Does it hold that temperature?
Grinding, rinsing the filter and weighing coffee takes three or four minutes, and an unheld kettle loses several degrees in that time. A published hold duration is the spec worth paying for.
Spout shape, and cone or flat bottom
A gooseneck exists to make the stream slow, narrow and aimable, and no manufacturer here publishes an inner spout diameter, so it is judged on published design alone. Below it, a V60 cone runs fast and rewards a steady pour; a Kalita flat bottom runs slower and forgives a clumsy one.
Counter cost, and what happens when it breaks
Everything here lives under any cabinet, but a kettle base still takes floor space — Fellow publishes 11.10 by 6.77 inches for the Stagg. A kettle is also an electrical appliance, so published warranty terms and a live manufacturer website are part of the purchase, and one brand here has neither.

Questions people ask

What temperature should water be for pour-over coffee?

SCA Standard 310-2021 specifies 90 to 96 °C, about 194 to 205 °F. Boiling water at 212 °F is above that band, so with a stovetop kettle, pour off after roughly 30 to 45 seconds off the heat.

How much coffee per cup for pour-over?

The SCA's standard brew ratio is 55 grams per kilogram of water — about 1 part coffee to 18 parts water, or roughly 19 grams for a single 350-gram brew.

Do I really need a gooseneck kettle, or will any kettle do?

A regular kettle dumps a wide fast stream that digs a hole in the bed and runs down the side of the filter. A gooseneck makes the stream narrow and slow enough to aim — the single component that changes a pour-over most, which is why half the picks here are kettles.

V60 or Kalita Wave — which should a beginner buy?

The Kalita, if you want consistency without practice: three small holes and a flat bed meter the flow for you. The V60, if you want to learn, because its single large hole hands all the control to your pour — and it is cheaper, with filters sold everywhere.
