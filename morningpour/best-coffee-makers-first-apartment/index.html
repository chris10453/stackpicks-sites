A first apartment is a set of constraints and none of them are about coffee. The counter is short and already holding a microwave. The cabinet above it is low enough that a rear-hinged lid will not open. And you might move again in eleven months, so whatever you buy has to survive a cardboard box.

So this guide ranks five ways to make coffee rather than five drip machines: the right answer for one person in a studio and for two who host on Sundays are not the same appliance. Every figure comes from the manufacturer's own page, and where a brand publishes nothing the table says so rather than guessing.

How to shop for a first coffee maker

What it costs to be wrong
Everything here is in the two cheapest price bands on this site, deliberately. The first coffee maker is the one you learn your own habits on, and half the people reading this will find in six months that they drink one cup, not a pot — a cheap lesson at these prices and a dear one at espresso-machine prices.
Counter footprint, and whether the cabinet is in the way
A rented kitchen gives you about 18 inches between the counter and the wall cabinet, per KraftMaid's published standard installation. A machine that fills from the front or has no lid at all makes that number irrelevant, which is worth more in a small kitchen than any feature on the box.
Whether it needs a plug and a circuit
Two of these five need no electricity at all, which matters in a kitchen where the outlet by the counter is already running a microwave and a toaster. US kitchens are required to have at least two 20-ampere small-appliance circuits, but in an older building you cannot assume both are where you want them.
How many people it feeds
Twelve cups is a pot for a household. Ten ounces is one mug. The most common first-apartment mistake is buying the 12-cup because it is the same price and then throwing away two-thirds of every pot — which is why the two smallest brewers here rank as high as they do.
What breaks, and what it costs to replace
A glass carafe is the part that breaks in a move. A plastic press body is not. Where a brand publishes a warranty we print it — BLACK+DECKER publishes two years, AeroPress publishes one — and where it does not, the table says not published.

Questions people ask

What is the cheapest way to make good coffee in an apartment?

An AeroPress Original and a bag of pre-ground coffee. It is in the cheapest band on this site, 5.25 inches tall, needs no electricity, and beats any drip machine at the same money because you control the water temperature and contact time instead of an unpublished thermostat.

Is a 12-cup coffee maker too big for one person?

Usually, yes — 12 cups is about 60 ounces at the 5-ounce cup BLACK+DECKER publishes, and one person drinks two. Both 12-cup machines here have a 1-to-4-cup setting that changes how water is dispersed over a small bed. If you never expect a guest, the AeroPress is the honest answer.

Do I need a burr grinder to start?

No, but it is the upgrade that changes the cup most — more than moving from a cheap drip machine to a dear one. A blade grinder produces dust and boulders in the same batch; our burr versus blade guide shows what that costs. Coffee maker first, grinder second.

How much counter space does a coffee setup actually need?

The narrowest full-size machine here is 7.95 inches wide. A pour-over setup — Chemex plus a kettle base — needs about 12 inches and no clearance above it. An AeroPress needs none, because it lives in a drawer. Measure the gap between counter and cabinet first.

Will a coffee maker and a microwave trip the breaker?

They can. A 20-ampere circuit at 120 volts carries 2,400 watts and a microwave alone is commonly 1,000 to 1,200. Neither 12-cup machine here publishes a wattage, which is the problem — a typical one draws 900 to 1,100 watts, so a shared circuit is close to the limit. The fix is a different outlet.
