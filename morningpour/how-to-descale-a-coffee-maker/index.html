How to descale a coffee maker, by brand

By Chris Y. · Founder & editor Updated September 12, 2026

There is no single correct way to descale a coffee maker, and the reason is simple: the manufacturers disagree with each other. Breville prints a vinegar recipe in its own manual — 4 cups of white vinegar to 8 cups of water. Technivorm says in writing that vinegar is not strong enough and will leave a taste, and tells you to use a descaling powder instead. Both are right about their own machines, and neither will help you with the other one.

So this page does not invent a universal method. It collects what seven brands publish for their own machines, in their own words and numbers, and links every one of them. Find your brand, use its instruction, and ignore everybody else's.

Two things are true across all of them. First, frequency is set by your water, not by the calendar — every brand says so, and a hard-water household descales two or three times as often as a soft-water one. Second, every method ends with running plain water through the machine until the smell is gone, and most people stop one cycle too early.

Questions people ask

Can I use vinegar in any coffee maker?

No. Breville prints a vinegar recipe in its own drip manual — 4 cups of vinegar to 8 of water — and Hamilton Beach specifies 2 cups undiluted. Technivorm says in writing not to use vinegar at all, because it is not strong enough and leaves a taste. Follow the brand that made your machine.

How often should I descale?

Technivorm says about every 100 brews or one box of filters. Keurig says every 3 to 6 months. Breville says monthly in soft water. De'Longhi and Cuisinart say when the light comes on. In hard water, take the shortest of those figures.

How many rinse cycles do I need afterwards?

Two to three full cycles of plain cold water, which is what Hamilton Beach, Breville and Keurig all specify. Let the machine cool between them. If the second pot still smells of vinegar, run a third — this is the step people cut short.

Is descaling solution better than vinegar?

For the machines whose makers recommend it, yes: descaling powders are formulated acids that break down calcium carbonate faster than acetic acid and rinse out without a smell. Technivorm's objection to vinegar is that it is too weak and too smelly, not that it is dangerous. For a cheap drip machine whose maker publishes a vinegar ratio, vinegar is fine.

Does descaling fix weak or bitter coffee?

Sometimes. Scale lowers the brew temperature and slows the flow, which under-extracts and makes coffee taste thin and sour. If descaling does not fix it, the next suspects are the grind and the ratio — our grind size chart and dialing-in guide cover both.

Breville: a vinegar recipe, in the manual

Breville is unusual in printing an exact vinegar quantity rather than selling you a sachet. The instruction book for the Grind Control drip machine tells you to empty the tank by running a full cycle with no coffee, then pour in white vinegar until the display reads 4 CUPS — about 20 oz — and top up with cold water until it reads 12 CUPS. That is four parts vinegar to eight parts water, a one-to-two solution.

You then hold the STRENGTH button for five seconds to start the descale cycle, which cannot be stopped once running. Afterwards you fill the tank to 12 CUPS with fresh water and run it through twice. Breville allows 30 to 40 minutes for the whole job and recommends doing it monthly in soft water and more often in hard water. On the espresso machines the machine tells you itself: the maintenance light or a scrolling dESC message is the prompt.

Technivorm Moccamaster: never vinegar

Moccamaster is the clearest counter-example, and the reason this page exists. Technivorm's own guide says plainly that it does not recommend vinegar — that it is not strong enough to break down mineral buildup and that it leaves a lingering odor and aftertaste. It names Urnex Dezcal and BioCaf descaling powders instead.

The method matters as much as the product. Dissolve the powder in 32 ounces of warm water in a separate container, never in the reservoir, because undissolved powder can clog the heating element. Never pour hot water into the brewer; if you used hot water to dissolve the powder, let it cool first. Then pour it into the reservoir and run a brew cycle with no filter and no coffee, and discard what lands in the carafe.

Technivorm's interval is the most specific one published by any brand here: about every 100 brews, or whenever you reach the end of a box of filters. In hard water, sooner. The reason they care is visible in their own photographs of the copper boiling element — a scaled one has almost no clear passage left through the middle.

Keurig: a whole bottle, and forty-five minutes

Keurig's use and care guide gives an interval of every 3 to 6 months, and says to descale promptly when the brewer displays the prompt itself. The procedure needs a large ceramic mug — not a paper cup — fresh water, a bottle of Keurig Descaling Solution and about 45 minutes.

You empty the reservoir and remove the water filter if fitted, pour in the entire bottle of descaling solution, then fill the empty bottle with water and add that too. Run cleansing brews at the largest size, with no pod, until the brewer asks for more water. Then leave the machine standing, switched on, for at least 30 minutes. After that, discard whatever is left, rinse the reservoir thoroughly and run fresh-water brews until the taste and smell are gone.

Hamilton Beach: two cups of vinegar, undiluted

Hamilton Beach publishes the simplest method of the seven, and the only one that uses vinegar neat. Pour 2 cups — one pint — of plain white vinegar into the reservoir of a standard drip machine and press BREW. After 30 seconds, press OFF. That short burst is the point: it pulls vinegar into the tubing and the heating element rather than straight through into the carafe.

Wait 30 minutes with the machine off, then press ON and let the cycle finish. Run two to three more full cycles with cold tap water only, letting the machine cool between them, until the vinegar is completely purged. On a single-serve FlexBrew the quantity changes to 14 oz and the stop-start is done with the BREW button instead.

Cuisinart: the CLEAN button, and two different published ratios

Machines with a CLEAN button do the timing for you. The instruction booklet for the PerfecTemp 14-cup tells you to remove the charcoal water filter first, fill the reservoir with one third white vinegar and two thirds water, then press and hold CLEAN until the indicator flashes. Five beeps mean the cycle is done. If the CLEAN light comes back on, repeat with fresh solution; otherwise run one full reservoir of cold water through to rinse, and put the charcoal filter back.

Worth knowing before you mix anything: Cuisinart's own web article on cleaning a coffee maker gives a weaker solution — three parts water to one part vinegar — than the booklet's one third to two thirds. Neither will hurt the machine, but they are not the same recipe, and on the day this page was written that article and the manuals index both returned a 404 to an automated request while the product pages loaded normally. Use the booklet that came with your model.

De'Longhi: wait for the orange light

De'Longhi builds the decision into the machine. Its guidance for the Dedica and Dedica Arte is to descale when the orange light on the button comes on, and for the automatic machines when the panel prompts you. The frequency, they say, depends on the hardness of your water rather than on a schedule — which is the same thing every other brand here says, expressed as a light instead of a number.

The product is EcoDecalk, diluted into the tank according to the instructions on the pack rather than to a ratio printed on the machine. De'Longhi sells it in a 16.9 oz bottle rated for five descales and in single-use twin packs.

BLACK+DECKER: read your own manual

BLACK+DECKER's published maintenance advice is the thinnest of the seven: clean the removable parts regularly — carafe, filter basket, reservoir — and descale as recommended in the user manual for your model. There is no universal solution, ratio or interval on the brand's site, and some models carry their own automatic clean cycle instead.

That is not a scandal, but it does mean the manual is the only source, and it is worth finding the PDF before the machine starts running slowly rather than after.

How to tell it is time

  • The brew takes longer. Scale narrows the tube the water travels through, so a machine that used to finish a carafe in six minutes and now takes nine is telling you something.
  • The coffee is cooler. Scale on the heating element insulates it. The water reaching the coffee bed arrives below the temperature the machine was designed around.
  • It is noisier. A scaled element boils unevenly, which is the gurgling and spitting people assume is normal.
  • There is white grit in the tank. By this stage you are late.

Three mistakes worth avoiding

  1. Using vinegar in a machine whose maker says not to. Technivorm is explicit about this, and so are several espresso manufacturers. The risk is not usually damage; it is a lingering smell and a weaker result.
  2. Under-rinsing. Every brand here ends with at least two full cycles of plain water. Vinegar in particular clings, and the first pot after a descale is the one that tastes of it.
  3. Leaving the water filter in. Cuisinart and Keurig both say to remove the charcoal or water filter before descaling. A filter left in place absorbs the solution and then releases it into your coffee afterwards.

What your water is doing

Every interval on this page is really a water-hardness figure in disguise. Harder water means more dissolved calcium and magnesium, which means faster scale, which means descaling two or three times as often as the manual's soft-water figure. It also changes how the coffee tastes before any scale forms at all — the Specialty Coffee Association publishes target ranges for hardness and alkalinity, and they are not the same in its two documents. We read both, from the source, in what the SCA water standard actually says.

If you are on hard water and you would rather solve the problem upstream, a filtered water supply reduces both the scale and the flat taste. Most drip machines here ship with a charcoal filter in the reservoir; it removes chlorine, not hardness, and it is not a descaling substitute.
