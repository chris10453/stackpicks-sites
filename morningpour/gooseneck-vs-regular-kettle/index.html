This comparison uses two kettles from the same brand and the same product line on purpose, because almost every gooseneck-versus-regular argument online is really an argument about temperature control. Both of these have it. Both have presets, both hold a temperature, both are Cosori Originals. Strip that out and one variable is left standing: the shape of the spout, and the capacity that comes with it.

What the spout actually changes is the rate and the aim of the water. A wide kettle spout dumps a fast, broad stream that digs a crater in the coffee bed, pushes grounds up the side of the filter, and puts most of the water through the middle. A gooseneck narrows it to something you can place. That matters enormously for a V60 and not at all for a teabag.

How to shop for an electric kettle

Stream width and where the water lands
A pour-over extracts evenly only if the whole bed gets wet at roughly the same rate. A wide spout cannot do that from a tilted kettle — the flow rate at the start of the pour is far higher than at the end, and the stream is wider than the area you are trying to wet. Neither manufacturer publishes an inner spout diameter, so this is judged on published design rather than a number.
Capacity, and what else the kettle has to do
The gooseneck holds 0.85 quarts — 0.8 liters — which is one or two pour-overs and nothing else. The glass kettle holds 1.7 liters, more than twice as much. In a kitchen with one kettle, that is the real trade: a specialist that pours beautifully or a generalist that fills a French press, a teapot and a saucepan.
Temperature control, which is not the variable here
Both kettles have presets and a hold. Cosori publishes five presets from 170 to 212 °F on the gooseneck with a 60-minute hold, and six presets with a 3 to 7 minute boil and a 60-minute keep-warm on the glass kettle. SCA Standard 310-2021 specifies 90 to 96 °C — 194 to 205 °F — and both reach into that band. This is the reason the comparison can be about the spout.
Time to temperature, against a full kettle
The gooseneck draws a published 1200 W into 0.8 L; the glass kettle publishes a 3 to 7 minute boil for 1.7 L. More water always takes longer, so the generalist is slower to a cup of anything even though it makes more of it.
What the water touches
Cosori publishes food-grade stainless throughout the gooseneck — kettle, lid and spout, with no plastic on the water path — and borosilicate glass with a 304 stainless filter on the glass kettle. Both carry a published two-year limited warranty. On this axis they are genuinely equivalent.

Questions people ask

Do I really need a gooseneck kettle for pour-over?

You need a controllable stream, and a gooseneck is the only practical way to get one from a kettle. A standard spout pours fast and wide: the water digs into the bed, channels through the middle, and runs down the paper instead of through the coffee. You can work around it by decanting into a jug, at the cost of the temperature you just set.

What temperature should the water be?

SCA Standard 310-2021 specifies 90 to 96 °C, which is about 194 to 205 °F. The gooseneck's 205 °F preset sits at the top of that band; the glass kettle's six presets cover it too. Water straight off a rolling boil at 212 °F is above the standard, so with a kettle that only boils, wait 30 to 45 seconds before pouring.

Is a 0.8-liter kettle too small?

For pour-over, no — a 350-gram brew uses less than half of it, and heating only what you need is faster. For a household kettle, yes: 0.8 liters will not fill a teapot and a French press in one boil, which is why the 1.7-liter kettle exists and why plenty of kitchens end up with both.

Can I just use a regular kettle and pour slowly?

Slower helps and it does not solve it. The problem is stream width as much as speed — a wide spout wets a wide area at once, so the bed cannot be agitated evenly or left alone deliberately. Tilting a full wide kettle far enough to trickle also puts the lid at an angle that drips.

Does a gooseneck kettle make the coffee taste better on its own?

Not by itself. It makes an even extraction possible, and an even extraction is what tastes better. If your grind is inconsistent — the usual cause of a bitter-and-sour cup at the same time — the kettle will not rescue it. Fix the grinder first; our burr versus blade comparison explains why.
