A no-pull harness does not stop pulling. It changes where the leash meets the dog, from the top of the shoulders, where a dog can put its whole weight against you, to the chest, where a step forward turns the dog back toward you instead of away. Everything else is fit.

And fit is a measurement problem, which is the reason this page exists: every harness below is bought by chest girth, every maker draws that measurement in a slightly different place, and one of the six publishes no adjustment count at all. Measure first, then read the rows.

How to shop for a no-pull harness

The published girth range, size by size
Chest girth is the only measurement that decides whether a harness fits. The ranges below overlap unevenly — PetSafe splits 12 to 49 inches across eight sizes, Ruffwear across five — and the more sizes a range is cut into, the less adjusting you are asking the straps to do.
How many points adjust
Four is the usual count, five and six are better, and every extra point is somewhere the harness can be made to sit right on a dog whose shape does not match the pattern. Deep-chested and barrel-chested dogs are where four-point harnesses fail.
Whether there is a front attachment at all
A back-clip harness on a puller gives the dog a sled-dog point of purchase. Every harness on this page has a chest ring; most also have one on the back, and the two used together is what trainers actually recommend.
What the strap does behind the front legs
This is where chafing happens, on the exact strap that takes the load when a dog lunges. Padding, lining and strap width are the three things makers do about it, and only two brands here publish a strap width.
Whether the maker says how to measure
"Measure the chest" is not an instruction. Blue-9 says ribcage, two inches behind the elbow. Ruffwear and PetSafe illustrate the girth point. The rest publish a number and leave you to guess where it is taken from, which is how a harness two sizes wrong arrives.

Questions people ask

Where exactly do I measure my dog's chest?

Around the widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs. Blue-9 is the most specific — around the ribcage about two inches behind the elbow — and that is the measurement its 13.5 to 43.5 inch range is built on. Take it with a soft tape, snug but not tight, and buy the smaller size when you fall between two.

Does a no-pull harness actually stop pulling?

It changes the geometry, not the behavior. Clipping at the chest means a dog that drives forward gets turned back toward you instead of gaining ground, which buys you the seconds in which training can happen. No harness on this page teaches a dog anything on its own.

Front clip or back clip?

Front for a dog that pulls, back for a dog that does not. Five of the six here have both — only the Easy Walk is front-only — and 2 Hounds and Blue-9 both recommend using the two together with a double-ended lead so the dog is redirected rather than braked.

How tight should it be?

Two fingers flat between the strap and the dog, everywhere, checked again after the first walk. This is where six points of adjustment earn their keep — with four, a dog whose build does not match the pattern ends up with one strap right and another wrong.

  • Prong and slip collars — Not compared here on method grounds. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior's position statement on humane dog training recommends reward-based methods and advises against training tools that work through discomfort.
  • Generic marketplace no-pull harnesses — Most publish one chest range and no measurement instruction, which is exactly the failure this page is built to avoid. A size chart without a stated measuring point is a guess with a table around it.
