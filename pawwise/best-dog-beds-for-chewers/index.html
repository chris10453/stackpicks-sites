The chew-proof bed category is built on a word nobody defines. Six makers here use it, one publishes a number, one publishes a warranty period, and the other four publish an adjective and a photograph of a dog looking pleased.

So this guide compares three things you can check: what the maker will commit to about chewing, what the frame is made of, and what happens if the dog wins. A bed with an aluminum frame and a 200-day replacement is a different purchase from a foam rectangle in a tough cover, and the price gap between them is smaller than you would think.

How to shop for a chew-resistant dog bed

A published chew rating
K9 Ballistics scores every bed out of ten for chew, scratch and water resistance, and scores its own cushioned beds as low as 6. A brand willing to publish a 6 is more useful than four brands publishing the word indestructible.
What the frame is made of, including the corners
On an elevated bed the fabric is the least interesting part. A dog works on the corner where the sling meets the leg, which is why K9 Ballistics and Kuranda both make a point of aluminum corners rather than plastic ones.
What the warranty covers when the dog wins
Kuranda publishes a one-year chew-proof warranty and excludes its own mesh fabric from it. Bully Beds publishes a 200-day chew warranty with one complete replacement. Both are more honest than any adjective, and both tell you the makers expect some dogs to win.
Whether the bed can go in the crate
Most chewing happens when a dog is left alone, and for many dogs that means in the crate. K9 Ballistics publishes the wire-crate size each bed fits and asks for a quarter inch of clearance in both directions; nobody else does this.
Whether it is a bed or a cot
An elevated sling is the durable answer and a hard one for an older dog with sore joints. The two cushioned beds here exist for that reason, and both are honest that they are for lighter chewers.

Questions people ask

Is any dog bed genuinely chew-proof?

No, and the most credible maker here says so. Bully Beds states that no material or bed exists that survives every dog, K9 Ballistics scores its best bed 10 and its cushioned beds 6, and Kuranda covers chewing for one year rather than forever. Buy the frame material and the warranty, not the phrase.

Elevated cot or cushioned bed?

Elevated for a chewer, cushioned for an older or sore dog. A taut sling with aluminum corners gives a dog nothing to start on; a foam bed always has a seam. The two cushioned beds here are scored or warranted accordingly.

What size bed fits in a 36-inch crate?

K9 Ballistics publishes this directly — its Medium at 35 by 22 inches is made for a 36 by 23 inch wire crate, with at least a quarter inch of clearance needed in both directions. Nobody else on this page publishes a crate-fit table, so for other brands measure the crate floor and subtract half an inch.

My dog destroys beds when left alone. Is a tougher bed the answer?

Partly. K9 Ballistics specifically warns against leaving its cushioned beds with a dog that shows separation-related destruction, which is a behavior question rather than a bedding one. A tough bed buys time; a veterinary behaviorist addresses the cause.

Also considered

  • Coolaroo elevated pet bed — A good cheap cot, and coolaroo.com redirects to an Australian store with no US product page to quote figures from. A US size and weight table could not be read from the maker, so it is named here rather than ranked.
  • Stuffed beds sold as chew resistant — A tough outer fabric over loose fill is a bed with stuffing in it. Every maker here that sells foam sells solid foam for exactly this reason, and two of them warn about swallowed filling.
  • Raised beds with plastic corner joints — The corner is where the sling meets the frame and where a dog starts. K9 Ballistics and Kuranda both make a point of aluminum corners; a cheaper cot with molded plastic ones is a different product wearing the same shape.
